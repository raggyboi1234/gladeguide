What is the Everglades?

The Everglades is a vast, shallow wetland in South Florida, often called the "River of Grass." Fed by rain and by water that once spilled south from Lake Okeechobee, it moves so slowly that it can look perfectly still. That gentle flow supports an unusual mix of habitats and wildlife found nowhere else in the country.

It matters far beyond the park boundary. Water soaking down through the Everglades helps refill the Biscayne Aquifer, a main source of drinking water for millions of people in South Florida, and its marshes hold and slow floodwater that would otherwise rush toward the coast.

Roughly half of the original Everglades has been lost to drainage for farms and cities, and the water that remains has been redirected by canals and levees. The Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan, authorized by Congress in 2000, aims to bring back a more natural flow. Neighborhoods like Weston sit right at the edge of the Everglades, so what happens in local yards and canals reaches the wetlands.

The Everglades has two seasons instead of four. In the wet season, roughly May through October, afternoon storms raise the water. In the dry season, roughly November through April, the water recedes and fish gather in shrinking pools, which is when wading birds feed most easily.

Six habitats, one system

Sawgrass marsh
Open stretches of sawgrass standing in shallow water for much of the year. This is the "grass" in River of Grass.
Tree islands
Small raised patches of trees and shrubs, called hammocks, that shelter wildlife when the water is high.
Cypress swamps
Stands of cypress rooted in water, used by wading birds for nesting.
Pine rocklands
Pine forests on limestone that depend on regular fire and shelter many rare plants.
Mangrove coast
Salt-tolerant trees where fresh and salt water mix, a nursery for young fish and a home for crocodiles.
Florida Bay
A shallow bay of seagrass at the southern edge, where manatees and many fish feed.

Why invasive species are a problem

A species is called invasive when it is not native to a place, spreads on its own, and harms the environment, the economy, or people's health. Most non-native species cause little trouble. The invasive ones are the exception, and they can be devastating.

South Florida is especially open to them. Warm weather all year, busy ports and airports, and large trades in exotic pets and garden plants bring new species in, and mild winters mean many survive. In the Everglades, many invaders began as pets that escaped or were released, or as ornamental plants that spread out of gardens.

Native species evolved together over thousands of years, and each helps keep the others in check. A newcomer arrives without its natural predators, diseases, and competitors, so it can multiply fast, and native animals often do not recognize it as a threat.

Why finding them early matters

Removing one animal or a few plants is far easier and cheaper than controlling a population that has already spread. That is why scientists rely on early detection and rapid response: when people spot something unusual and report it quickly, managers can act before it takes hold.

What you can do

  • Never release a pet

    Releasing an unwanted pet or dumping an aquarium is how many invaders start. Florida's Exotic Pet Amnesty Program lets you give up a pet with no penalty.

  • Choose native plants

    Plant species that belong here, and remove known invasive plants from your yard so their seeds and berries do not spread into natural areas.

  • Protect your pets

    Bring pet food indoors at night and keep pets away from unfamiliar toads, frogs, and reptiles.

  • Clean your gear

    Seeds and spores can hitchhike on boots, boats, and trailers. Clean them before you leave a natural area.

  • Learn and report

    Use the Identify page to check what you have found, then report it with a photo and a location.
